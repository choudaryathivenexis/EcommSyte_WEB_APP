TACoS vs ACoS: The Metric That Actually Predicts Profitable Growth

If I could delete one number from most sellers' brains, it would be ACoS in isolation. Not because it's useless — it's genuinely helpful — but because it's the metric people optimize when they should be watching something bigger. Let's fix that.

The two-minute definition

ACoS (Advertising Cost of Sales) is ad spend divided by the sales those ads directly generated. Spend $200, make $1,000 in ad sales, and your ACoS is 20%. It's an efficiency metric — it answers "how hard did this ad work?"

TACoS (Total Advertising Cost of Sales) is ad spend divided by your total revenue — ads plus organic. Spend that same $200, but your total sales are $2,000, and your TACoS is 10%. It's a health metric — it answers "how dependent is my brand on paid traffic?"

Why ACoS lies to you

ACoS can look beautiful while your business quietly shrinks. Pause every campaign and your ACoS drops to zero — congratulations, and also, your sales just fell off a cliff. It can also look terrible during a launch when you're spending aggressively to buy rank you'll keep for months.

The problem is that ACoS only sees the ad-attributed slice of your business. It's blind to the organic sales your advertising helps create. And on Amazon, ads and organic aren't separate systems — they feed each other. Paid velocity lifts your rank, better rank drives organic sales, and organic sales reinforce rank again.

What TACoS actually tells you

Watch TACoS over time and it narrates the real story of your brand:

  • TACoS falling while revenue rises — the dream. Your organic engine is getting stronger and you're leaning less on ads for each dollar. This is what healthy scaling looks like.
  • TACoS flat while revenue rises — fine, and normal during expansion. You're buying growth at a steady rate.
  • TACoS climbing while revenue is flat — the warning light. You're paying more to stand still, usually a sign of rising competition or a slipping listing.

That single trendline catches problems months before they show up in your bank balance.

ACoS tells you how a campaign performed. TACoS tells you whether your brand is actually getting healthier.

So which one do I optimize?

Both — for different jobs. Use ACoS at the campaign and keyword level to decide where to cut waste and where to push. Use TACoS at the brand and product level to judge whether the whole strategy is working. One is your scalpel; the other is your compass.

A trap to avoid: chasing a low TACoS by starving your ads. If TACoS is 4% and sales are stagnant, you're probably under-investing — leaving rank and shelf space for competitors to take. A slightly higher TACoS that's buying real growth beats a low one that's buying nothing.

Setting targets by stage and category

There's no universal "good" TACoS, but here's the framework we use with clients:

  1. Launch phase — expect 20–40%+. You're deliberately overspending to build rank and reviews. Judge success by ranking gains, not efficiency.
  2. Growth phase — aim for 10–18% and trending down. The organic base should be taking more of the load each month.
  3. Maturity / defense — many established brands settle at 6–12%, depending on how contested the category is.

Fiercely competitive categories — supplements, phone accessories, generic home goods — run higher because the ad auction is brutal. Differentiated or branded products run leaner because organic demand does more of the work. Set your target against your own trend and your category, never against a number you read in a forum.

A quick worked example

Picture two supplement brands, both doing $100,000 a month. Brand A runs a 25% ACoS and is thrilled — clean, efficient campaigns. Brand B runs a 32% ACoS and frets about it constantly. On the surface, Brand A looks like the better operator.

Now add the missing context. Brand A's ads drive $60,000 of that revenue, so its TACoS is 15% — and it's been flat for six months. Brand B's ads drive $40,000, so its TACoS is roughly 13% — and it's been falling steadily while total sales climb. Brand B is the healthier business by a mile. Its organic engine is doing more of the work every month, and its slightly higher ACoS is buying rank that keeps paying off. Brand A is simply efficient at treading water.

That's exactly the misread TACoS prevents. Efficiency in isolation is a comfortable story. Health, tracked over time, is the truth.

One more nuance worth internalizing: ACoS and TACoS should move in a sensible relationship. If your ACoS holds steady while TACoS keeps falling, your organic sales are compounding beautifully — keep doing exactly what you're doing. If both climb together, something structural has shifted in the category or the listing, and it's time to dig into the cause rather than just trimming bids and hoping.

Make it a monthly ritual

The sellers who use this well don't calculate TACoS once and forget it. They build it into a simple monthly review: total revenue, total ad spend, TACoS, and the direction of travel for each hero product. Fifteen minutes a month is enough to catch a slipping listing, a rising competitor, or an under-invested winner long before any of it reaches your P&L. The metric is only powerful if you actually watch the trend — a single snapshot tells you almost nothing.

Putting it to work this week

Open your business reports and calculate TACoS for your top five products over the last six months, month by month. Plot it next to revenue. In ten minutes you'll see which products are becoming self-sustaining and which are quietly getting more ad-dependent. That picture tells you exactly where to dig — and it's the same lens our PPC team uses on every account we manage.

Key takeaways

  • ACoS measures ad efficiency; TACoS measures how dependent your brand is on paid traffic.
  • Falling TACoS with rising revenue is the clearest sign of healthy, compounding growth.
  • Use ACoS to optimize campaigns and TACoS to judge whole-brand strategy.
  • Don't starve ads to force a low TACoS — under-spending quietly hands rank to rivals.
  • Set targets by stage: high at launch, trending down through growth, lean at maturity.
